WELCOME

Welcome to the Guide

PTSD does not only affect the person who experienced the trauma. It can also affect the people who live with, love, and support them.

For some Veterans, coming home after experiencing trauma does not mean everything immediately goes back to normal, it takes time and it is necessary for their loved ones to know this. PTSD symptoms may include anxiety, irritability, emotional numbness, sleep problems, withdrawing from everyone, avoiding certain situations, or feeling constantly alert.

These symptoms can affect their everyday life and relationships.

A spouse may feel confused, lonely, worried, frustrated, or emotionally tired. They may wonder why their partner has changed or why communication has become impossible.

Research has shown that military spouses living alongside a serving or Veteran partner with a mental health problem may experience caregiver burden and relationship and emotional challenges (Senior et al., 2023).

Why I Created This Guide

I created this guide because I wanted to make information about PTSD and relationships easier to understand.

I also wanted to remind spouses that supporting their partner is important, but taking care of themselves is important as well and they shouldn't neglect their wellbeing.

Who Is This Guide For?

This guide is intended for:

  • Veterans living with PTSD symptoms
  • Spouses and intimate partners
  • Family members
  • Caregivers
  • Anyone who wants to better understand/knowledge about PTSD and relationships

UNDERSTANDING PTSD

What Is PTSD?

Post-traumatic stress disorder, also called PTSD, is a mental health condition that can develop after someone experiences or is exposed to a traumatic event.

For Veterans, trauma may be connected to military service, combat, serious injury, death, violence, or other frightening experiences.

Not everyone experiences PTSD in the same way. Symptoms can also change over time (Public Health Agency of Canada [PHAC], n.d.).

Some Common PTSD Symptoms

A person may experience:

  • Unwanted memories or thoughts about the trauma
  • Nightmares
  • Flashbacks
  • Avoiding people, places, or situations
  • Being easily startled
  • Feeling constantly alert or on guard
  • Problems sleeping
  • Irritability or anger
  • Feeling emotionally numb
  • Feeling disconnected from others
  • Difficulty concentrating
  • Negative thoughts or emotions
5

PTSD Can Look Different From Person to Person

One person may become quiet and withdrawn.

Another person may become angry or easily irritated.

Someone else may avoid crowds, social events, or situations that remind them of the trauma, while another may remain indoors or isolated.

Understanding PTSD can help a spouse understand what may be happening, but it does not mean the spouse should accept harmful behaviors.

Remember

PTSD can explain some behaviors, but it does not excuse abuse, threats, or violence.

Both partners deserve safety, respect, and support.

WHEN PTSD COMES HOME

When the Person Comes Home but the Trauma Comes with Them

Coming home after military service or a traumatic experience does not always mean that a person feels completely at home again.

A Veteran may be physically home while still dealing with the effects of trauma.

PTSD can affect communication, emotional connection, family life, and intimate relationships (Larsen, n.d.; Veterans Affairs Canada [VAC], n.d.).

A Spouse May Notice:

  • Their partner becoming quieter
  • Irritability or anger
  • Problems sleeping
  • Being easily startled
  • Avoiding crowds or social activities
  • Spending more time alone
  • Difficulty talking about feelings
  • Less affection
  • Changes in intimacy
  • More arguments
  • Emotional distance

A Spouse May Feel:

  • Lonely
  • Confused
  • Rejected

PTSD AND COMMUNICATION

When Talking Becomes Difficult

Communication can become difficult when someone is dealing with PTSD.

Avoidance, emotional numbness, irritability, anger, sleep problems, and feeling constantly alert can make it harder to communicate (Larsen, n.d.; PHAC, n.d.).

It May Look Like:

  • Arguments over small things
  • Misunderstandings
  • One person withdrawing
  • Difficulty talking about feelings
  • Anger
  • Avoiding difficult conversations
  • Feeling emotionally disconnected
  • Feeling rejected
  • Feeling overwhelmed

This does not always mean that the relationship is failing. Sometimes these difficulties may be connected to the effects of trauma.

8

What Can Help?

1. Choose the right time.

Do not try to have an important conversation when one person is already very upset. As this may cause further damage.

2. Keep your words simple.

Try to explain how you feel without attacking the other person.

Instead of: "You never listen to me."

Try: "I feel like I am not being heard."

3. Listen without immediately trying to fix everything.

Sometimes a person wants to be heard before they are given advice.

4. Respect boundaries.

If someone needs a short break, give them space while also agreeing to come back to the conversation when everyone is calmer.

5. Get professional support when needed.

Couples therapy can help couples understand their difficulties, communicate, solve problems, rebuild trust, and feel closer (VAC, n.d.).

Try Asking:

  • "What is one thing I can do that would help you feel heard?"
  • "What do you need from me when you are having a difficult day?"

TRUST AND EMOTIONAL CONNECTION

When You Feel Far Apart

PTSD can sometimes create emotional distance between partners.

Avoidance and emotional numbness may make it difficult for someone to connect emotionally or physically with their partner (Larsen, n.d.).

The Veteran may be withdrawing because they feel overwhelmed, anxious, angry, or emotionally numb.

The spouse may see the withdrawal and think:

"My partner does not love me anymore or my partner is seeing someone else."

This can create even more distance.

Some Challenges May Include:

  • Less communication
  • Less quality time together
  • Less affection
  • Emotional distance
  • Misunderstandings
  • Trust issues
  • Loneliness
  • Feeling unwanted

Research has found an association between PTSD and relationship satisfaction and intimacy difficulties (Sijercic et al., 2022).

10

Rebuilding Connection Can Start Small

You do not have to fix everything at once.

Try:

  • Having a short conversation every day
  • Eating a meal together
  • Taking a walk
  • Watching a movie
  • Having a simple check-in
  • Showing appreciation
  • Saying thank you
  • Spending quiet time together
  • Giving each other personal space

Ask Yourself:

What is one small thing we can do this week to feel connected again?

INTIMACY AND RELATIONSHIPS

Intimacy Is More Than Sex

When people hear the word intimacy, they may think about sex.

But intimacy can also include:

  • Emotional closeness
  • Affection
  • Trust
  • Communication
  • Touch
  • Feeling safe
  • Being able to share your feelings

PTSD can affect relationship satisfaction and emotional and physical intimacy. It may also affect sexual functioning and desire (Larsen, n.d.; Sijercic et al., 2022).

A Couple May Experience:

  • Less interest in intimacy
  • Avoiding touch
  • Difficulty feeling emotionally close
  • Changes in sexual desire
  • Anxiety around intimacy
  • Difficulty talking about needs
  • Feeling rejected
  • Feeling unwanted
12

What Can Help?

Talk about it.

Do not assume that a change in intimacy automatically means your partner does not love or want you.

Build emotional safety.

Start with communication and connection.

Respect consent and boundaries.

Both partners should feel comfortable saying yes or no rather than assuming.

Ask for help.

Professional support may help couples understand what is happening and work on their relationship.

Research suggests that individual and couples-based PTSD treatments can improve PTSD symptoms and may also improve intimate relationship functioning (Sijercic et al., 2022).

SUPPORTING YOUR PARTNER WITHOUT LOSING YOURSELF

You Matter Too

Supporting someone with PTSD can be difficult.

A spouse may become so focused on helping their partner that they sometimes forget about themselves.

But you cannot pour from an empty cup, you have to be okay to help someone else.

Veterans Affairs Canada recognizes the important role families and caregivers can play while also recognizing the importance of support for families themselves (VAC, n.d.).

Ways You Can Support Your Partner

  • Listen
  • Encourage professional help
  • Learn about PTSD
  • Be patient when possible
  • Communicate honestly
  • Spend quality time together
  • Encourage healthy routines
  • Respect personal space
  • Set healthy boundaries

Ways to Take Care of Yourself

  • Take time for yourself
  • Talk to someone you trust
  • Spend time with friends or family
  • Rest
  • Do things you enjoy
  • Ask for help
  • Consider counselling
  • Maintain healthy boundaries
14

Ask Yourself:

  • Am I taking care of myself too?
  • Do I have someone I can talk to?
  • Have I been putting my partner's needs above my own all the time?
  • Do I feel safe in my relationship?

Remember

Supporting your partner does not mean tolerating abuse.

You can love someone and still have boundaries.

You can support someone and still say:

"This behavior is not okay."

A STORY OF TWO PARTNERS

Story about Josh and May

"I Miss the Person I Used to Know"

This is a fictional story. Josh and May are fictional characters and are not based on one specific Veteran or couple.

Josh returned home after his military service.

He was happy to be home, but things did not feel the same, he felt off but yet he couldn't place it.

He was having problems sleeping. Small noises sometimes startled him. Crowded places made him feel uncomfortable. He often sat alone and did not want to talk to anyone not even May.

May noticed the changes.

One evening she asked:

"Are you okay?"

Josh replied:

"I'm fine."

May knew he was not fine.

She began to feel like Josh did not want her around anymore, this made her feel sad and unwanted.

One day she said:

"I feel like you don't want me around anymore."

Josh became quiet.

16

He was not trying to hurt May. But he was having difficulty explaining what he was feeling.

Eventually, they began to understand that both of them were being affected by this recent development.

Josh was struggling with the effects of trauma.

May was struggling with loneliness, confusion, and worry.

They decided to seek professional support.

They also started having shorter and calmer conversations instead of trying to solve everything during an argument.

May learned that supporting Josh did not mean ignoring her own needs.

Josh learned that communicating what he was feeling could help May understand him better.

Their relationship did not change overnight.

But they began to understand that they were not enemies.

They were two people trying to understand what trauma had brought into their relationship.

What Can We Learn From Josh and May?

  • PTSD can affect both partners.
  • A spouse may experience loneliness and confusion.
  • A Veteran may have difficulty explaining what they are experiencing.
  • Communication is important.
  • Both people may need support.
  • Asking for professional help is not a failure.
  • Supporting your partner should not mean losing yourself.

Reflection

How can couples create a safe space where both partners feel comfortable expressing their needs and emotions?

WHAT CAN COUPLES DO?

A Simple 5-Step Check-In

You do not have to solve every problem in one conversation.

Try this:

1. STOP

Always remember to pause before reacting.

2. CHECK

Ask yourself: "Am I calm enough to have this conversation?"

3. LISTEN

Give the other person a chance to speak without interrupting.

4. EXPRESS

Use:

  • "I feel..."
  • "I need..."
  • "I would like..."

5. PLAN

Ask: "What can we do differently next time?"

18

Try a Weekly Check-In

Once a week, ask each other:

  • What went well this week?
  • What was difficult?
  • How are you feeling emotionally?
  • Is there anything you need from me?
  • What is one thing we can do together this week?

The goal is not to solve everything.

The goal is to understand each other.

Remember

Small changes can make a difference.

You do not have to fix everything today.

WHEN TO SEEK HELP

Knowing When You Need More Support

Remember, there is nothing wrong with asking for help.

Professional support may be helpful when PTSD symptoms or relationship difficulties begin to interfere with everyday life.

Consider Seeking Help If There Are:

  • Frequent arguments
  • Ongoing communication problems
  • Difficulty managing anger
  • Serious sleep problems
  • Increased isolation
  • Problems at work or home
  • Ongoing relationship distress
  • Problems with intimacy
  • Feelings of hopelessness
  • Difficulty managing PTSD symptoms
  • Concerns about safety

Support May Include:

  • Individual counselling
  • Couples counselling
  • Trauma-focused therapy
  • Mental health services
  • Medical assessment
  • Peer support
  • Family support
  • Veteran-specific services
20

Most Important

You do not have to wait until things become a crisis before asking for help.

Getting help does not mean that you failed.

It means you recognize that you need support.

If Someone Is in Immediate Danger

Call 9-1-1 or go to the nearest emergency department.

If you or someone you know is thinking about suicide or worried about someone else, call or text 9-8-8 in Canada. The 9-8-8 Suicide Crisis Helpline is available 24/7.

FINAL THOUGHTS

You Are Not Alone

PTSD can affect more than the person who experienced the trauma.

It can affect communication, trust, emotional connection, intimacy, family life, and the overall relationship (Larsen, n.d.; Senior et al., 2023).

But PTSD does not mean that a relationship cannot grow.

Understanding what is happening can be a starting point.

Communication can be a starting point.

Asking for help can be a starting point.

Taking care of yourself can be a starting point.

To the Veteran

You do not have to go through this alone.

It is okay to ask for help.

To the Spouse

Your needs matter too.

Supporting your partner does not mean losing yourself.

You are allowed to rest.

You are allowed to ask for help.

You are allowed to have boundaries.

You are allowed to seek safety.

To Both Partners

Healing does not always happen overnight.

Take one step at a time.

RECOMMENDATIONS

Based on the information in this guide, there are some important recommendations for veterans, spouses, families, and mental health professionals.

For Veterans

  • Learn about PTSD and how it may affect your thoughts, emotions, behaviour, and relationships.
  • Talk to your partner when you feel able to instead of keeping everything inside.
  • Consider professional support when PTSD symptoms are affecting your daily life or relationships.
  • Remember that asking for help is not a sign of weakness.
  • Take responsibility for your behaviour. PTSD may explain some behaviours, but it does not excuse threats, abuse, or violence.

For Spouses and Partners

  • Learn about PTSD so you can better understand what your partner may be experiencing.
  • Remember that supporting your partner does not mean forgetting about yourself.
  • Make time for rest, friends, family, hobbies, and other things that support your own well-being.
  • Set healthy boundaries and speak up when something is not okay.
  • Seek support when you feel overwhelmed, isolated, unsafe, or unsure about what to do.

For Families and Caregivers

  • Encourage the Veteran to seek professional help when needed.
  • Avoid trying to manage everything alone.
  • Learn about available Veteran and family support services.
  • Remember that caregivers also need support and time to care for themselves.

For Addiction and Mental Health Professionals

  • Consider the needs of both the Veteran and the spouse when providing support.
  • Provide clear education about PTSD and its possible effects on relationships.
  • Encourage healthy communication, self-care, boundaries, and appropriate professional support.
  • Connect Veterans and their families with community and Veteran-specific resources.
  • Always consider safety when there are concerns about violence, threats, or abuse.

Final Recommendation

PTSD can affect more than the person who experienced the trauma. It can also affect the people closest to them. Veterans and their spouses should be encouraged to seek support early, communicate openly when possible, protect their well-being, and remember that they do not have to face these challenges alone.
